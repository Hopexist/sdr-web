QPSK: a complete digital link

What am I looking at?

QPSK and Gray coding. Each symbol carries two bits as one of four phases, (±1 ± j)/√2, normalized so the average symbol energy Es = 1. Gray coding puts bit pairs that differ in one bit next to each other, so the most likely mistake (to a neighbour, 90° away) costs one bit, not two. That gives the classic BER = Q(√(2·Eb/N0)).

RRC and the matched filter. Symbols are shaped by a root-raised-cosine pulse, and the receiver filters with the same pulse. Together they make a raised cosine, which is zero at every other symbol instant (no inter-symbol interference), and the matched filter maximizes SNR at the sampling instant. The roll-off β trades bandwidth ((1 + β)·Rs, the shaded band on the spectrum) against how forgiving the eye is to timing errors.

The eye diagram overlays two-symbol slices of the filtered signal. A wide-open "eye" at the strobe means clean decisions; noise closes it vertically, timing jitter horizontally, and a residual carrier rotation smears I into Q.

Coarse CFO by the 4th power. Raising a QPSK sample to the 4th power maps all four points to the same value (−1), wiping out the data and leaving a pure tone at 4× the frequency offset. One FFT finds it.

Gardner timing recovery. With two samples per symbol (the strobe and the midpoint between symbols), the Gardner detector Re{m*·(yk−1 − yk)} tells whether we sample early or late, and it works before the carrier is fixed. A loop filter steers an NCO-style strobe, and a cubic Farrow interpolator produces samples at the exact fractional times. Its gain Kp is measured from the S-curve.

The carrier PLL compares each symbol with its nearest constellation point (decision-directed) and steers a phase/frequency estimate with a second-order loop. Because QPSK looks the same after a 90° turn, the PLL can lock with a 0/90/180/270° error. Correlating with the known 64-symbol preamble finds the frame start, and its phase reveals which of the four it is. Differential encoding sidesteps the problem instead, at about twice the BER.

Loop bandwidth. BnT sets how fast each loop reacts. Wide loops lock quickly and follow drift, but pass more noise (jittery estimates, even cycle slips). Narrow loops are quiet but slow, and may never catch up with a frequency offset or clock drift. Try the "Loop too wide" and "Loop too narrow" presets.

Fading. When the signal arrives over many scattered paths, they add with random phases and the received gain h(t) is complex Gaussian: its magnitude is Rayleigh distributed (Rician when a line-of-sight path dominates, with K = LOS power / scatter power). In AWGN, BER falls exponentially with SNR. Under Rayleigh fading it falls only as 1/(4·SNR), because the errors come from the occasional deep fades, not from the average SNR. The "time below −10 dB" readout tracks those fades.

Doppler and coherence time. Motion makes h(t) change at a rate set by the Doppler shift fd. The channel stays roughly constant for a coherence time of about 0.4/fd. Pilots spaced well inside that time let the receiver interpolate h between them (pilot-aided). A preamble-only estimate is fine for block fading but goes stale as soon as the channel moves. Pilots cost throughput, which is why the overhead is shown.

Multipath and equalization. Echoes arriving a symbol or more late smear symbols into each other (ISI), which shows as notches in the channel's frequency response. An equalizer is a filter that undoes it. Zero-forcing inverts the channel exactly, which at a deep spectral null means huge gain, so it boosts the noise there (try "3-tap severe" with ZF). LMS adapts towards the minimum-MSE solution: it trains on the known preamble, then follows its own decisions, and accepts a little ISI to avoid amplifying noise. CMA needs no training at all. It only pushes |z| towards 1, so it leaves a phase rotation for the PLL to fix. The equalizer sits after timing recovery (it needs symbol-spaced samples) and before the carrier PLL (a decision-directed PLL can't lock on ISI). Once converged, channel × equalizer should be flat.

Phase noise vs loop bandwidth. A real oscillator's phase random-walks. The carrier PLL must be wide enough to follow it, or the phase drifts by 90° and a cycle slip corrupts everything after it. But every bit of extra bandwidth lets more thermal noise into the phase estimate. The best BnT balances the two.

IQ imbalance is a gain or phase mismatch between the I and Q mixers. It adds a mirror image of the signal: y = μ·x + ν·x*. The image rejection ratio |μ|²/|ν|² says how strong it is, and it appears as a squashed, skewed constellation. Because a proper QPSK signal has equal, uncorrelated I and Q power, the receiver can measure the imbalance blindly and re-orthonormalize the axes (Gram–Schmidt).

ADC bits. Every bit of resolution adds about 6 dB: SQNR ≈ 6.02·N + 1.76 dB for a full-scale sine, minus the backoff the AGC leaves for peaks. Too little backoff and the peaks clip (watch the histogram pile up at ±FS); too much and the signal uses only a few quantizer levels.
